Data pressure through the ages

  • Every era's model was the right bet for its bottleneck
  • Today's document patterns were bets placed in the NoSQL era
Ported from the 'From RDBMS to NoSQL' deck (its timeline slide, same ten builds) and reframed: the point isn't nostalgia, it's that every era's model was a correct bet against that era's bottleneck.

▸ Frame it before the first click (this was the slide's lede): every era's dominant model was the right bet for that era's bottleneck. The patterns we argue about weren't wrong. They were bets placed against a constraint that has since moved. Each peak on the curve is data pressure exceeding the technology of the day; each valley is the solution that relieved it, until the next peak.
▸ Build 1 · Human brain (~100,000 BC): the first database. Memory is the only store: it doesn't scale past one head and it doesn't survive the holder.
▸ Build 2 · Ledgers (~3000 BC): write it down. The first durable, shareable record: the valley that relieved memory.
▸ Build 3 · Punch cards (1890s): Hollerith's tabulator for the 1890 US census: data becomes machine-readable, and volume starts to outrun people.
▸ Build 4 · Magnetic tape (1950s): storage at scale, but sequential: fast to write, slow to find. To read record N you pass records 1 to N−1.
▸ Build 5 · Block storage (1970s): random-access disk makes navigation possible. The hierarchical engines of the day (IMS) optimized for scarce storage and navigation by pointer: you followed the path the designer built, and only that path.
▸ Build 6 · RDBMS (1980s): Codd traded storage for correctness: normalize, store each fact once, join at read. Any question, not just the pre-built path. The valley.
▸ Build 7 · Distributed NoSQL (2000s): web scale outgrew one machine. NoSQL traded correctness for scale-out on cheap disk: denormalize, avoid joins. Set the hook here: the document patterns we use every day were invented at this peak: bets against a distributed engine that couldn't join, couldn't transact across shards, and re-serialized a whole record to touch one field. Hold that thought.
▸ Build 8 · Polyglot persistence (2015+): the highest peak. A separate store per access dimension (SQL, document, graph, time series, vector), each with its own copy of the facts and its own consistency model. That's the next slide but two.
▸ Build 9 · Unified Model Theory (2020s): the deepest valley. One canonical form, projected into every shape the consumers need. The constraint lifts: one source, every shape.
▸ Build 10 · the line on the slide: each peak = data pressure exceeding current technology; each valley = the solution.
▸ The key move (was the slide caption): IMS optimized for scarce storage and pointer navigation. Codd traded storage for correctness. NoSQL traded correctness for scale-out. Each move solved the previous era's pain, and inherited a new one. None was wrong; each was a bet.

Why we ever denormalized: the economics flipped

  • Then: disk was expensive, so store each fact once
  • Now: CPU, network hops and wait time are expensive
  • Keep one truth; let the engine maintain the read shapes
Explain WHY we denormalize. Most people do it by reflex without knowing the economics changed under them.

▸ Then: storage was dear, so you stored each fact once and paid joins to reassemble. Codd's normal forms are, at heart, a deduplication discipline.
▸ Now: storage is nearly free; the dear resources are CPU, network hops, and human milliseconds. So you pre-shape for the read: denormalize at read time.
▸ The move the industry missed (cool card): the right response to cheap storage isn't 'throw away the normalized truth'; it's 'keep it AND spend cheap storage to materialize the shapes consumers want.' Denormalization becomes a projection, not a fork.
▸ Walk the graphic: two cost bars, then and now, and they swap. Then, storage was the expensive bar and compute was cheap enough, so the answer was normalize: store each fact once and join to reassemble it. Now storage is nearly free and the expensive bar is compute and latency: CPU cycles, network hops, human milliseconds.
▸ The lede, said out loud: normalization is a storage-saving technique from an era when storage was the expensive resource. That era ended. But the reflexes it created, and the reflexes the next era created, are still driving models.
▸ The callout arrow: denormalize → project (engine-maintained). The right response to cheap storage isn't 'throw away the normalized truth.' It's 'keep the normalized truth, and pay cheap storage to also materialize the shapes each consumer wants.' Denormalization becomes a projection, not a fork. You get read-time shape without giving up write-time correctness, because the engine maintains both.
▸ Codd's normal forms are, at bottom, a deduplication discipline, and denormalization was always about spending the cheap resource to save the dear one. The mistake was making it a one-way, hand-maintained copy instead of an engine-maintained projection.

Polyglot persistence: five stores, five versions of the truth

  • Same fact, five stores, five different sync delays
  • An AI agent acts on whichever copy it reads
This is where 'purpose-built' gets its comeuppance, and where AI turns a tolerable flaw into an intolerable one.

▸ Three costs (hot cards): integration tax (serialize + hop + auth on every cross-store call), the consistency gap (stores converge eventually, disagree in between), and the AI failure mode (stale + conflicting facts → confident hallucination → bad action).
▸ State Vector Dissonance (bottom card): the vector index reflects the last embed job; the tables reflect this transaction. RAG retrieves from the first and reasons as if it were the second. The wider the gap, the more confident the wrong answer.
▸ The turn: a human reading a slightly stale answer shrugs. An agent taking an action on it creates a liability.
▸ The lede, said out loud: 'purpose-built' sounds disciplined. In practice it means the same fact lives in five engines with five consistency models, and nothing owns the reconciliation. That was tolerable when a human read the answer. It is not tolerable when an AI acts on it.
▸ Walk the diagram left to right: at 10:00:00 one fact changes: customer tier = Gold. It fans out to five copies: document store, search index, vector index, graph store, cache. Every hop on that fan-out is the integration tax (serialization, a network hop, an auth handshake), and chains of them dominate latency and become the thing you spend your quarter debugging.
▸ The clocks are the eventual consistency gap: each copy catches up on its own schedule: milliseconds for the document store, seconds for the search index, the next embed job for the vector index, a 10-minute expiry (TTL, time to live) for the cache. Five seconds after the write, two of five copies still say Silver. Which copy did the model just read?
▸ The AI failure mode: the agent's RAG retrieval hits the vector copy, reads Silver, and denies a Gold customer's fee waiver. Feed a model stale and conflicting facts and it doesn't error; it confidently hallucinates. When it then takes an action, the stale context becomes a business liability.
▸ State Vector Dissonance (was the bottom card): the vector index reflects the world as of the last embed job; the operational tables reflect the world as of this transaction. RAG retrieves from the first and reasons as if it were the second. The wider the gap, the more confident the wrong answer.

Land: AI didn't create this flaw. It removed our tolerance for it. Immediate consistency across vectors, documents, graph and relational stops being a nicety and becomes a correctness requirement.

Act 2 · The physics0:29 – 0:31

The breakpoint: when precomputing stops paying

Every "should I maintain this precomputed shape?" decision reduces to one inequality. Precompute only when the reads you save outweigh the writes you add.

What moves the right side up: more writes, fatter documents, worse write amplification: a subset trimmed on every write to serve a read that rarely happens.

What a cost-based optimizer does: it lowers read_cost (an index-only Top-N, a hash join, a parallel scan), so the inequality holds at a shape you never had to hand-maintain.

A better optimizer moves the breakpoint. Patterns that "must" be precomputed on a document engine can be queried live on a converged one, because the read side just got cheaper.

Reduce the whole 'should I precompute?' debate to one inequality. This is the tool they'll actually use in design reviews.

▸ Read it aloud: maintain a precomputed shape only if read_freq × read_cost > write_freq × maintenance_cost. What you save on reads must beat what you pay on writes.
▸ What moves the right side up (hot card): more writes, fatter docs, worse write amplification, e.g. a subset trimmed on every write to serve a read that rarely happens. That's the inequality flipping.
