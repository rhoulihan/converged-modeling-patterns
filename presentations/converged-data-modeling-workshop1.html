The choice was never "relational or document"

  • Relational vs. document was never a team to join
  • Model once, project many: the workload picks the shape
Kill the false binary first. Every bad decision downstream rests on it.

Say the lede out loud: for 20 years we have argued data models like teams to join. They were never teams. Relational and document are projections of the same truth, each shaped by what the engine of the day could afford. Change the engine and the argument changes.

The left panel is the old frame. Normalize here, denormalize there, buy a purpose-built store per access pattern (document store, search index, graph store, vector index, relational database) and glue them together. Point at the glue line: every joint charges an integration tax: serialize, network hop, auth handshake, on every call. Every joint opens a consistency gap: the copies disagree until they catch up. Every document pattern is a coping strategy for one engine's limits. The arrow is the reframe: the fix isn't better glue. It's fewer copies.

The right panel is today's frame: model once, project many. One canonical form, the logical model of entities and relationships, each fact modeled once. The engine stores it as projections (rows on tables, documents, graph nodes and edges, vector indexes); each consumer's query reads its shape in one transaction, under one optimizer. The cost of choosing it goes, because the engine maintains every shape from one source. Don't oversell it: reading a projection still does work at read time. What you stop paying is write amplification and hand-built sync code, and the cost-based optimizer does the optimizing.

Act 1 · Why converged, why now0:05 – 0:09

Data pressure through the ages

  • Every era's model was the right bet for its bottleneck
  • Today's document patterns were bets placed in the NoSQL era
Every era's dominant model was the right bet against that era's bottleneck. Frame that before the first click. The timeline comes from the 'From RDBMS to NoSQL' deck (same ten builds); the point isn't nostalgia. The patterns we argue about weren't wrong. They were bets placed against a constraint that has since moved. Each peak is data pressure exceeding the technology of the day. Each valley is the solution that relieved it, until the next peak.

Build 1, the human brain (~100,000 BC): the first database. Memory doesn't scale past one head, and it doesn't survive the holder.

Build 2, ledgers (~3000 BC): write it down. The first durable, shareable record, the valley that relieved memory.

Build 3, punch cards (1890s): Hollerith's tabulator for the 1890 US census. Data becomes machine-readable; volume starts to outrun people.

Build 4, magnetic tape (1950s): storage at scale, but sequential. Fast to write, slow to find. To read record N you pass records 1 to N−1.

Build 5, block storage (1970s): random-access disk makes navigation possible. IMS and its hierarchical peers optimized for scarce storage and pointer navigation: you followed the designer's path, and only that path.

Build 6, the RDBMS (1980s): Codd traded storage for correctness. Normalize, store each fact once, join at read. Any question, not just the pre-built path. The valley.

Build 7, distributed NoSQL (2000s): web scale outgrew one machine, and NoSQL traded correctness for scale-out on cheap disk. Denormalize, avoid joins. Set the hook: today's document patterns were invented at this peak, as bets against a distributed engine that couldn't join, couldn't transact across shards, and re-serialized a whole record to touch one field. Hold that thought.

Build 8, polyglot persistence (2015+): the highest peak. A separate store per access dimension (SQL, document, graph, time series, vector), each with its own copy of the facts and its own consistency model. Slide 6 takes it apart.

Build 9, Unified Model Theory (2020s): the deepest valley. One canonical form, projected into every shape the consumers need. The constraint lifts: one source, every shape.

Build 10, the line on the slide: each peak is data pressure exceeding current technology; each valley is the solution.

Then the key move, the old caption. IMS optimized for scarce storage. Codd traded storage for correctness. NoSQL traded correctness for scale-out. Each move solved the previous era's pain and inherited a new one. None was wrong. Each was a bet.

Land: hold that thought. The UMT valley lifts exactly the constraints that produced the NoSQL-era patterns, which is what makes those patterns optional.

Why we ever denormalized: the economics flipped

  • Then: disk was expensive, so store each fact once
  • Now: CPU, network hops and wait time are expensive
  • Keep one truth; let the engine maintain the read shapes
Most people denormalize by reflex, without knowing the economics changed under them. This slide is the why.

Say the lede out loud: normalization is a storage-saving technique from an era when storage was the expensive resource. That era ended. The reflexes it created, and the reflexes the next era created, still drive models.

The graphic is two cost bars, then and now, and they swap. Then, storage was the expensive bar and compute was cheap enough, so you normalized: store each fact once, pay joins to reassemble it. Codd's normal forms are, at bottom, a deduplication discipline. Now storage is nearly free and the expensive bar is compute and latency: CPU cycles, network hops, human milliseconds. So you pre-shape for the read and denormalize. Denormalization was always about spending the cheap resource to save the dear one.

The callout arrow is the move the industry missed: denormalize becomes project, engine-maintained. The right response to cheap storage isn't 'throw away the normalized truth.' It's 'keep it, and spend cheap storage to also materialize the shapes each consumer wants.' Denormalization becomes a projection, not a fork. You get read-time shape without giving up write-time correctness, because the engine maintains both.

Land: the mistake was never denormalizing. It was making it a one-way hand-maintained copy instead of an engine-maintained projection. That distinction is the entire deck.

Act 1 · Why converged, why now0:15 – 0:18

Polyglot persistence: five stores, five versions of the truth

  • Same fact, five stores, five different sync delays
  • An AI agent acts on whichever copy it reads
'Purpose-built' sounds disciplined. This is where it gets its comeuppance, and where AI turns a tolerable flaw into an intolerable one. The three hot cards carry it.

Say the lede: in practice, purpose-built means the same fact lives in five engines with five consistency models, and nothing owns the reconciliation. That was tolerable when a human read the answer. It is not tolerable when an AI acts on it.

Walk the diagram left to right. At 10:00:00 one fact changes: customer tier = Gold. It fans out to five copies: document store, search index, vector index, graph store, cache. Every hop is the integration tax (serialization, a network hop, an auth handshake), and chains of them dominate latency and become what you spend your quarter debugging.

The clocks are the eventual consistency gap. Each copy catches up on its own schedule: milliseconds for the document store, seconds for the search index, the next embed job for the vector index, a 10-minute TTL (time to live) for the cache. Five seconds after the write, two of five copies still say Silver. Ask the room: which copy did the model just read?

Then the failure. The agent's RAG retrieval hits the vector copy, reads Silver, and denies a Gold customer's fee waiver. Feed a model stale, conflicting facts and it doesn't error. It confidently hallucinates. A human reading a slightly stale answer shrugs. An agent acting on it turns stale context into a business liability.

The bottom card names it: State Vector Dissonance. The vector index reflects the world as of the last embed job. The operational tables reflect the world as of this transaction. RAG retrieves from the first and reasons as if it were the second. The wider the gap, the more confident the wrong answer.

Land: AI didn't create this flaw. It removed our tolerance for it. Immediate consistency across vectors, documents, graph and relational stops being a nicety and becomes a correctness requirement.

Unified Model Theory: the vocabulary for the rest of the day

  • UMT = Unified Model Theory: Codd's idea, made operational
  • Five terms; every decision today has a place in one
Give them five words. Every modeling decision in the next hour lives in one of them, so make them stick.

Say the lede: UMT is Codd's insight, made operational and given names. Then state the flow in one sentence. The Canonical Form is stored as Projections; queries run against an Access Surface, carry an Access Dimension, and turn the projections into the Projected Shape the consumer reads. It is the query, not the storage, that makes the shape.

Read the boxes left to right. Canonical Form: the logical model, the entities, their properties and their relationships, modeled to the domain, not the screen. Projections: the physical representations of that same data, rows on tables, documents in JSON collections or duality views, nodes and edges in a property graph. Access Surface: the interface the consumer queries through, SQL, SQL/JSON, SQL/PGQ, the MongoDB API. Projected Shape: what comes back, a document, a graph, a vector result, a rowset. The blue arrow over the top is that step: queries on the access surface render the shape. Access Dimension: the kind of question the query asks, point read, range, aggregate, traverse, similarity.

Then walk it back up from the query. It carries its Access Dimension, arrives at an Access Surface, runs against the projections and renders the Projected Shape. Every projection describes the one Canonical Form.

Now the correction people need most. The canonical form is not the tables. Relational tables are one projection of it, exactly like a JSON document or a property graph. Normalization is how we keep the logical model free of repeated facts; the tables are one place we store it. No hand-waving from here on. Every decision has a precise home.

Land: 'Model once. Project as documents, graphs, time series, or relations. Serve every consumer from one source of truth.' That's the whole theory; the rest is physics and tradeoffs. (This is the money quote from the RDBMS-to-NoSQL deck; say it verbatim.)

Three knobs decide every model

Strip away the vocabulary and every modeling decision (embed or reference, precompute or query) turns on three measurable quantities. Set these, and the model is half-chosen for you.

Diversity of access · read/write ratio · update % and where it lands. High diversity wants projections. Read-heavy wants precomputation. But knob 3 can veto both, which is where the patterns come from.

The hinge. Before: why. After: these three knobs.

Knob 1 is diversity: how many distinct access patterns hit this data? High diversity wants projections; one shape can't serve them all. Knob 2 is the read/write ratio: read-heavy justifies precomputation, write-heavy punishes it. Knob 3, the red dial, is update rate and locality: where the write lands inside the shape, not just how often. That is write amplification, and document modeling under-weights it.

The caption: knob 3 can veto knobs 1 and 2. A read-heavy, high-diversity workload still can't embed if the embedded field is hot and mutable.

Land: set these three and the model is half-chosen. Every pattern coming up is a response to a particular setting of knob 3.

Why the patterns exist: write amplification

Every document pattern in the catalog is, underneath, a reaction to one physical fact: in a document engine, touching one field can rewrite the whole record. Understand this and the patterns stop being folklore.

…then every copy pays it again: × replicas (HA)high-availability copies × regions / cloudsdisaster-recovery copies × backups & snapshotsrewritten pages stored again × change streams / CDCchange data capture feeds

The bigger and hotter the document, the more every write costs. Bucket, Computed, Subset, Pre-Allocation: they all exist to keep documents small and writes cheap. They are write-amplification management, dressed up as design patterns.

Slow down. Get write amplification across and every later pattern explains itself.

Update one field and a copy-on-write engine re-serializes the entire leaf page. Write cost scales with document size, not change size. Make it visceral: a 2 MB document costs ~2 MB of write to change one byte. Every time. Add a document-level lock and the march toward the 16 MB cap.

The multiplier strip: that rewrite isn't paid once. Every replica (HA, high availability) re-applies it to its own page, every cross-region or cross-cloud copy pays again, backups and snapshots capture the rewritten pages, and change streams and CDC (change data capture) ship it, often the whole post-image document, to every consumer. One-byte edit, 2 MB × every copy.

The reveal: Bucket, Computed, Subset and Pre-Allocation all exist to keep documents small. They're write-amplification management dressed up as design patterns.

Act 2 · The physics0:29 – 0:31

The breakpoint: when precomputing stops paying

Every "should I maintain this precomputed shape?" decision reduces to one inequality. Precompute only when the reads you save outweigh the writes you add.

What moves the right side up: more writes, fatter documents, worse write amplification: a subset trimmed on every write to serve a read that rarely happens.

What a cost-based optimizer does: it lowers read_cost (an index-only Top-N, a hash join, a parallel scan), so the inequality holds at a shape you never had to hand-maintain.

A better optimizer moves the breakpoint. Patterns that "must" be precomputed on a document engine can be queried live on a converged one, because the read side just got cheaper.

'Should I precompute?' reduces to one inequality, the tool they'll use in design reviews.

Read it aloud: maintain a precomputed shape only if read_freq × read_cost > write_freq × maintenance_cost. What you save on reads must beat what you pay on writes.

A trigger, not a fast-refresh view: what each costs per reading

  • Both keep the hourly summary exact with zero staleness; they pay in very different places
  • The view log alone outweighs the whole trigger; the commit-time refresh is the rest
Keep this one in your back pocket. Someone will ask why the lab hand-writes a trigger when Oracle has fast-refresh materialized views.

Both are right answers to the same question. Each keeps a running summary per machine and hour, exact, with zero staleness, and the companion lab checks both against a GROUP BY. The difference is where they pay. The trigger pays inline: one MERGE into the summary row per reading, 375 bytes of redo and 14 microseconds on top of a 594-byte insert. The view pays twice. Every insert also writes a row to the materialized view log, 784 bytes on its own, already more than the trigger's entire bump. Then every commit runs the fast refresh, which reads the log, applies the deltas and purges it: 6,639 bytes and 1.27 milliseconds per reading when each reading commits alone.

Read the two panels together. At one reading per commit the view costs 8 times the redo and 40 times the time. Batch 10 readings per commit and the refresh amortizes: 3.4 times and 7.7 times. The refresh is a per-commit tax. Batching softens it; it never makes the view cheaper than the trigger.

Be fair to the view. It is declarative, it needs no code, and the optimizer can rewrite a matching GROUP BY to read it. For a summary that changes slowly, or a batch load that commits rarely, that convenience is worth paying for. For an IoT stream that commits every reading, it is the most expensive way to get the same numbers.

Measured on 26ai Free in the companion lab: scratch tables, insert-only readings, two runs averaged, both summaries verified equal to the GROUP BY.

Land: declarative convenience has a price at ingest rates. Measure it before you pick it.
